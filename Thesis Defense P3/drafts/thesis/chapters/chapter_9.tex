\section{Summary of Findings}

This research set out to turn a recorded Bengali-English code-mixed whiteboard lecture into
a lecture note a student can use. The system was built as four stages and each stage was
measured separately.

Off-the-shelf speech recognition does not work on this speech, and the failure is
specific. On six held out lectures Whisper large-v3-turbo produces a median character
error rate of 67.7 per cent and a median word error rate of 93.9, translating the speech
into English instead of transcribing it and falling into repetition loops on thirteen of
the 177 test clips. A small adapter on the same model corrects most of that. Trained on
4.10 hours of hand-checked Banglish from 22 lectures, LoRA adapters bring the character
error rate down to 15.8 and 16.0 per cent in two random seeds and the word error rate to
42.5 and 41.7, better on 164 and 167 of 177 clips and needing no decoding safeguard. The
gain also holds for a lecturer the model has never heard, at a lower level, cutting the
character error rate from 72.8 to 50.3 per cent in all six runs. Training at the selected
settings is not stable, however, since one of two seeds at the chosen learning rate
collapsed into repetition and produced output worse than doing nothing.

The whiteboard can be rebuilt from the video without inventing anything, and the median
board has 97.7 per cent of its tiles fully clear. A human check of 98 boards found 82
complete, with writing genuinely lost on 9 and the remaining 7 explained by the source
video and not by the method. Reading the board is then a question of prompting rather
than of model size. The same vision-language model on the same images moves from 31.2 to
88.8 per cent recall of hand-verified board content when the prompt asks for a full
transcription instead of keywords, and from none of 67 numbers to 65 of 67, while a model
less than half the size reaches 85.4 per cent. Board content can be made to reach the
notes, which carry 89.1 per cent of the board items against 37.2 for a conventional
summarisation prompt.

Several approaches did not work, and that is part of the result. Dual recogniser fusion
gives 0.7 percentage points at p = 0.32, and biasing the decoder towards board words halves
term recall at every non-zero strength. Using occlusion as a pointer is not supported, at
p = 0.054 and p = 0.159. The display clean-up makes the model read boards very slightly
worse, and fine-tuning the speech model does not measurably improve board-content recall
in the notes.

Measures built out of English strings mislead in this setting, and this appeared twice
independently. A term based F1 over a fixed English word list swings 8.1 points between
two training runs on identical data and moves against transcription quality, because a
better Banglish transcript contains fewer English words. In the note evaluation the
transcript with 67.7 per cent character error contains significantly more of the English
record items than the one with 15.8 per cent, for the same mechanical reason.

\section{Contributions to the Field}

Stated as narrowly as the evidence allows, the contributions are the following. They are
ordered by how far each one reaches beyond this dataset, because the corpus is the most
visible contribution but not the most transferable one.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A demonstration that the prompt, rather than the model size, is what makes a
        vision-language model read a handwritten board. On the same 35 boards with the
        same model, changing only the prompt moved board-content recall from 31.2 to 88.8
        per cent, while going from the 3 billion parameter model to the 7 billion
        parameter one moved it from 83.7 to 89.0. The prompt is worth 57.6 points and the
        model size 5.3, about eleven times less. The prompt was re-checked on a held-out
        half of the boards, where it gives 28.4 to 84.7 per cent, so the conclusion does
        not rest on the boards the prompt was chosen on. Nothing about this finding is
        specific to Banglish or to this corpus.

  \item A note-quality measure that a language model cannot satisfy from prior knowledge.
        Board-content recall scores only facts physically written on the board, which the
        summarising model cannot invent from what it already knows, so it separates
        grounding from world knowledge. It behaves as a valid measure should: recall falls
        as items become more guessable. Generated lecture notes are usually evaluated by
        fluency or by overlap with a reference summary, and neither can tell whether the
        note is about the lecture that was actually given.

  \item A measured demonstration that measures assembled from English strings are invalid
        for code-mixed speech, because they reward a model that translates and penalise
        one that transcribes. The inherited term measure moves in the wrong direction as
        transcription improves, since better Banglish emits fewer of the English words the
        measure counts, and it moves by 8.1 points between two runs on identical data.
        This was observed twice in unrelated measures within one project and it agrees
        with the published literature on code-switched evaluation.

  \item The first measurement, as far as could be found, of what published Bengali and
        Bengali-English speech models actually produce on spontaneous code-mixed classroom
        speech. Five models were run on the same held-out clips as this work. Not one
        produced romanised Banglish on a single clip: four write Bengali script and the
        fifth translates into English. The finding survives the obvious objection, since
        transliterating their output to Roman and scoring again still leaves the best of
        them at 47.1 per cent character error against 15.8 here.

  \item A hand-checked corpus of code-mixed classroom speech in romanised Banglish.
        Forty-four lecture recordings totalling 8.33 hours, of which 28 lectures and 5.15
        hours are transcribed word by word against the audio according to a written
        standard, with 609 timed segments and about 39,100 words. No comparable corpus of
        spontaneous Bengali-English classroom speech transcribed in the Roman alphabet
        could be found, and the nearest published corpora in this language pair are
        synthetic.

  \item A measured speech recognition result for this variety under a strict evaluation
        design. Whole lectures are held out, references are human, the settings come from
        a procedure written down before any run, and two seeds are reported for every
        configuration including the one that diverged. Two designs are reported and
        labelled, one with the test lecturers heard in training and one with an entirely
        unseen lecturer.

  \item A whiteboard reconstruction that generates nothing, read into text by a
        vision-language model and scored against hand-verified records. The reconstruction
        itself is engineering assembled from known parts, and what is new is the
        combination with a vision-language reader and the measurement of it, including 45
        boards and 436 items verified by hand and 98 boards checked for missing writing.

  \item A note generator whose claims can be checked. Board content is supplied as text
        instead of being recalled, non-lecture material is confined to a labelled box,
        quotations are verified word for word against the transcript and deleted when they
        fail, and references to numbered regions of the board are checked against the
        regions that exist. Each of these is counted in the output file of every page.

  \item A set of measured negative results, since fusion, decoder bias, occlusion as a
        pointer and the display clean-up were all built, all measured and all reported as
        they came out.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Recommendations for Future Work}

The reader study reported in Section \ref{sec:reader-study} is a pilot and the obvious
thing to extend. Twenty readers on one lecture preferred the notes this system produces,
but the comparison moved several things at once and no reader was asked to learn from
either note. A fuller study would randomise the order, cover several lectures and
lecturers, separate the changes so that the board images, the box references and the
language can be judged one at a time, and above all ask readers to answer questions about
the lecture after studying a note, which measures learning rather than preference. A
second arm should compare notes built from the off-the-shelf transcript with notes built
from the fine-tuned one, which is the only way to test the part of the speech improvement
that board-content recall cannot see.

Measuring whether the generated prose is true is still untouched. A factual error has
already been found by reading, and nothing in this research counts how often that happens.
The check needs a reader who knows the subject going through the notes statement by
statement against the recording.

One smaller piece of evidence would also strengthen the report.
Timing transcribers as they correct real model drafts would replace the projection in
Section 3.8 with a measurement, and it is the number a department would actually want.

On the speech side, more data from under-represented lecturers is the clearest next step,
since the lecturer with 0.7 hours in training has a test lecture at 52 per cent character
error while the others reach 12 to 16, and collecting more from that lecturer would test
whether the cause is the data or the speaker. A scaling curve on the current corpus,
planned and cancelled when the corpus closed, would answer how much more transcription is
worth buying. The divergence at the selected learning rate is reproducible enough to
study, and warmup, gradient clipping or a lower scaling factor are the obvious things to
try; a configuration that never diverges would be worth more than a small gain in error
rate. Reporting the spelling tolerant variants of Section \ref{sec:asr-spelling} as
standard, and applying the same idea to the note measure, would reduce the measurement
problem described throughout this research.

On the vision and note side, four changes have known causes. Holding an era open until a
wipe finishes would fix the four boards cut in the middle of an erase. Ignoring a region
that appears unchanged in every era of a lecture would stop the box finder putting a
numbered box around a sticker. Evaluating the box finding would attach a number to the one
part of the pipeline that has none, and it needs layout ground truth that does not yet
exist. Notes in the Bengali script were dropped from this research deliberately, since they
would be produced by translation alone, and they are straightforward to add if a reader
wants them. A larger notes model was intended for comparison and dropped for disk space;
it would change the writing rather than the facts, since it reads the extracted board text
and never the board.

The system does what it was built to do, and this report states how well. A student can
put in a recorded Banglish whiteboard lecture and get out a note containing the lecturer's
words and the lecturer's board, with the board drawn from real pixels and the quotations
checked against the transcript. The speech model is good enough to be a draft and not good
enough to be trusted unchecked, the board reading is strong and the prompt is the reason,
and whether the notes are genuinely useful to a student is the one question this research
cannot answer.
