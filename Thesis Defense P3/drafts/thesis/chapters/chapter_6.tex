random, 20 per cent of each lecturer's minutes with the seed fixed at zero, giving 22
training lectures totalling 4.10 hours and six test lectures totalling 1.05 hours and 177
clips. The three validation lectures used for tuning were locked out of the test set.

The instability in the last row of Table \ref{tab:asrfinal} has to be reported with the
result. At 2e-3, the learning rate the pre-registered procedure chose on the validation
lectures, one of the two seeds collapsed: 82 of the 177 clips produced runaway output and
the character error rate reached 118.7 per cent, which is worse than doing nothing. The
data, the split and every other setting were identical to the seed that reached 16.2 per
cent, so the 16.2 per cent figure is never quoted in this thesis without its failed twin.
The same configuration has shown this fragility before, since LoRA rank 32 and all four
attention projections both diverged during tuning.

The 1e-3 rows also require a disclosure. The pre-registered procedure selected 2e-3, and
the runs at 1e-3 were launched after the divergence had been seen, so that choice was not
blind and 1e-3 is not presented as the pre-registered winner. What can be claimed is
narrower. The runner-up rate from the same tuning table is stable across two seeds on the
test set while the chosen rate is not. The selection rule itself was not changed and
remains recorded in the committed tuning plan.

The procedure chose a learning rate of 2e-3 with rank 16, the query and value projections
and 8 epochs. That configuration is 8.6 character error points better than the starting
point, and the acceptance rule required it to beat the default by more than the 5.4 point
spread between two seeds.

Three findings follow from this table. More adapter capacity hurts at this learning rate,
since rank 32 diverged outright and adapting all four attention projections diverged as
well, so five hours of speech does not support more trainable parameters. Validation loss
is a poor guide to the metric that is reported. The loss is lowest at 5 epochs, at 1.666
against 1.801 at 8 epochs, so stage 4 retrained there and the character error rate got
worse, from 33.6 to 39.4 per cent. And the margin is not large next to seed noise, at 8.6
points ahead of the default with a 5.4 point spread from a single seed change, measured on
34 minutes of validation speech. It passes the rule written in advance while remaining a
modest effect, which is consistent with the divergence that the same configuration later
showed on the test set.

A rehearsal of the procedure had been run earlier on 2.69 hours of transcript to check
that the machinery worked. It chose the same learning rate and produced the same two
divergences.

An earlier result in this project, on a smaller model, only beat its baseline when
Whisper's compression-ratio loop safeguard was switched on, so the obvious challenge to
the present result is that the safeguard is doing the work. Rescoring both final models
with the safeguard gives 15.8 per cent for seed 42 and 16.0 per cent for seed 1, identical
to the greedy figures to the first decimal, because the fine-tuned model loops on one clip
in 177 and the safeguard has nothing to rescue. Off-the-shelf Whisper loops on thirteen.
All figures reported in this thesis are the plain greedy ones.

Banglish has no standard spelling, so a reasonable challenge is that the word error rate
is inflated by \textit{aami} against \textit{ami}. Two spelling tolerant variants were
computed on the same saved outputs with the rules committed before any rescoring. These
come from the six leave-one-speaker-out runs rather than from the final model, because

medians, plain word error rate moves from 95.3 to 75.7 per cent between the base and the

accepts a word at 80 per cent character similarity from 92.7 to 68.2. The canonical
spelling table therefore moves the word error rate by about one point and near-miss
spellings by about seven more, so most of the word error is genuine recognition error.
The fine-tuned model is better in all six runs on all three measures, with p between
2.2e-16 and 2.7e-06.

\subsection{An unseen lecturer, and a correction to an earlier result}
\label{sec:asr-loso}

are also heard in training. A harder question is what happens on a lecturer the model has

with two seeds per fold and plain greedy decoding.

Averaged over the six runs the character error rate falls from 72.8 to 50.3 per cent and
the word error rate from 95.3 to 75.7 per cent. All six runs are significant under plain
decoding and runaway clips fall or stay level in every fold. Fine-tuning on 80 to 114
minutes of transcribed Banglish from two lecturers therefore cuts the character error on
a third, unheard lecturer by about a third, for every one of the three lecturers and in
both seeds. It does not follow that this is usable speech recognition, since a 50 per
cent character error rate is still very high, nor that the finding holds across many
speakers, since three is a small number. Both designs are labelled every time they
appear: Section \ref{sec:asr-result} is new lectures from known lecturers on 4.10 hours,
and this one is an unseen lecturer on 80 to 114 minutes.

An earlier version of this work reported a word error rate falling from 96.1 to 81.8 per
cent on a speaker never seen in training. That claim was wrong. The original nine
transcripts carried no lecturer label, so the lectures were labelled by hand and one was
attributed to the wrong lecturer. That lecture was in the training set and its lecturer
was the test speaker, so 47 clips of the supposedly unseen speaker had been trained on.
The error was found by computing speaker embeddings, which separate the groups without
ambiguity at 0.99 to 1.00 similarity within a lecturer and 0.75 to 0.80 across. The result
was recomputed on the corrected split, the old number was retired, and the voice check is
now a standing part of data preparation.

A second check concerns the malformed timestamps described in Section 4.3.2. Both models
were scored on the same clips, so the comparison is fair, but the effect was measured
rather than assumed. Without the ten affected clips the result is 72.2 per cent to 49.6
per cent character error against the published 72.8 to 50.3, so the defect made the
published figure slightly pessimistic and the published figure stands.

\subsection{Rebuilding the board}
\label{sec:board-coverage}

The reconstruction is measured first by coverage, the fraction of tiles that found a
moment when the lecturer was not in front of them. Across the 35 boards of the first nine
lectures the median board has 97.7 per cent of its tiles fully clear, the mean is 96.5 per
cent and the range is 87.5 to 100. Six boards reach 100 per cent, 26 of the 35 reach 95
per cent or better, and only two fall below 90. Over all 145 boards produced in the
project the median is 100 per cent and the mean 98.4, with the worst board at 76.6.

Short eras give a tile fewer chances to be seen clear, and six eras are under eight
frames. A lecturer who stands in one place is the other cause, and the worst lecture in
the set is the one where the lecturer holds a single position longest. That second cause
was addressed rather than excused. Extracting frames every 2 seconds instead of every 10
raised the number of his boards reaching 95 per cent clear from 1 to 5 out of 10 and
improved the worst board from 59 to 88 per cent.

Coverage measures the method rather than the result, and it should not be read as
fidelity. A tile can be clear while the writing is still missing, because the writing may
never have been visible in any frame. Answering that needs a person, so every one of the
98 boards of the newer lectures was checked by hand, with each rebuilt board shown beside
two real frames from the same period and the question being whether the board contains
everything that was written.

The nine real losses are small. Seven are a single token or one diagram, such as a label
\texttt{t4}, the number 100 or the word Hybrid, and one board is the bad case, where a
whole subnetting tree is absent. Counting only the boards whose source frames were usable,
which drops the four mid-erase eras and the three out-of-focus ones, the reconstruction is
complete on 82 of 91 boards, or 90.1 per cent. The four mid-erase cases have a different
cause and a different fix. The era boundary was placed while the lecturer was still
wiping, so every frame in that era shows a half erased board, and holding the era open
until the wipe finishes would correct it.

These three sets of numbers count different things and should not be added together.
Coverage counts tiles, the human check counts whether writing survived, and the recall
figures below count whether a model could read the writing back.

\section{Analysis of Design Solutions}

This section analyses the design choices against the alternatives. Four of the
measurements below are negative results.

\subsection{Three approaches that did not work}

An English recogniser and a Bengali recogniser were run on the same nine lectures and
their outputs merged. The merged output scores 73.9 per cent on the term measure against
the baseline's 73.2, a difference of 0.7 percentage points, with an exact paired
permutation test at p = 0.32 and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test at p = 0.36. Four of the nine
lectures get worse, and the word error rate of the merged output is 146.8 per cent against
the baseline's 82.6. Fusion does not fix Banglish recognition, which is what motivated
fine-tuning instead.

A sweep of the decoder bias strength from 0 to 2.0 on one lecture gave term recall of 8.8
per cent with the bias off and 4.1 per cent at every non-zero setting, with the transcript
truncated from 279 to 149 characters. Every non-zero strength produced byte-identical
output. The shape of that result matters more than its size. This is not a tuning problem
with a better setting waiting to be found, because the mechanism fails as soon as it
engages. The recorded output gives the optimal bias as zero, so the experiment concluded
against itself.

Where the lecturer stands might indicate what the lecture is currently about, which would
give a free alignment between speech and board regions. The test needs no manual labels:
if standing in front of a region means working on it, that region should gain more ink
after the lecturer leaves than the regions he was not standing in front of. Over 191
occlusion episodes in nine lectures the occluded region gained more ink in 101 cases and
an unoccluded region in 81, with a Wilcoxon test at p = 0.054 and an exact sign test at p
= 0.159, which does not support the claim. The most likely reason is that the test
measures writing, while a lecturer standing in front of a region is often explaining it
and adding no ink at all, which is exactly the case where the signal would have been most
useful.

\subsection{Ablations on the vision stage}
\label{sec:board-cleanup}

The clean-up makes every board a clean white image and was judged by eye to be better.
Read by the same vision-language model with the same prompt against the same records, it
is slightly worse. Over the 35 boards of the first nine lectures the mosaic scores 334 of
349 items and the cleaned board 329, and over the third lecturer's ten boards 74 of 87
against 71. Combined over 45 boards that is 93.6 per cent against 91.7, better on 1 board
and worse on 7, with a sign test at p = 0.0703.

The difference is not significant either way, so the statement supported by the data is
that there is no measured difference, with the point estimate against the clean-up.
Reading the changes item by item explains the shape. The one gain is on the board the
clean-up was built for, where an end-of-era diagram that the old mask lost becomes
readable and the board rises from 57.1 to 71.4 per cent. The losses are all single fine
details of the kind a clean-up erases, being long code strings, a written out sentence and
two decimal numbers. There is also very little room to win, since the mosaic is already at
95.7 per cent on the first nine lectures and 37 of the 45 boards score identically on
both.

The cleaned boards are therefore kept for how they look in a note rather than because they
help the model read, no claim is made that the rebuild improved board reading, and the
headline board figures are reported on the mosaic boards.

\label{sec:vlm-size}
Two further claims the design invites were tested in the same way, by changing one thing
and rescoring. The first is that the result needs a large vision-language model. Board
reading was run again with Qwen2.5-VL-3B, changing nothing but the model. Over the
35 boards of the first nine lectures the 3B model scores 85.4 per cent against the 7B's
88.8, and over the third lecturer's boards 77.0 against 89.7. Combined over 45 boards and
436 items that is 83.7 per cent against 89.0, with the 7B better on 16 boards and worse on
6 and a sign test at p = 0.053.

Changing the prompt is worth 57.6 percentage points, from 31.2 to 88.8. Changing the model
from 3B to 7B is worth 5.3 points, and that difference is not significant by the sign test
on either set on its own. A 3B model at 85.4 per cent already carries most of the board.
The 7B is the better model and remains the one reported, but model scale is not what made
board reading work, which agrees with the published finding that large multimodal models
are strongly sensitive to how the instruction is written
\cite{ismithdeen2025promptception}. The 32 and 72 billion parameter versions were not run:
their weights are 68.3 GB and 146.8 GB, the machine had 62 GB of free disk at the time,
and both would have needed quantising to fit in 32 GB of video memory, which would
confound model size with quantisation loss. This is therefore a comparison of a 3B model
against a 7B model rather than a scale study.

The second claim is that the reconstruction improves reading, and the measurement does not
support it. On the 35 boards of the first nine lectures, recall rises from 88.8
per cent on a raw frame to 95.7 on the rebuilt board, better on 8 boards and worse on 3,
with a sign test at p = 0.23 and a Wilcoxon test at p = 0.056. On the third lecturer's
boards it moves the other way, from 89.7 to 85.1. This is a trend on one set and absent on
the other, so the prompt is what makes the model read the board and the reconstruction is
what makes the board usable in a note. Two limits of the reconstruction were exposed by
the hand check. Glare from the ceiling lights sits in every frame, so no choice of frame
removes it, and content visible in only one frame can be lost, which happened to a binary
number that was fully written at one moment, covered ten seconds later and wiped ten
seconds after that.

\subsection{Does fine-tuning the speech model improve the notes?}
\label{sec:2x2}

This question joins the two halves of the thesis and the answer is no, in a way that needs
explaining rather than hiding. Four sets of notes were built for the 13 scored lectures,
Banglish only, with everything held constant except whether the transcript came from the
fine-tuned or the off-the-shelf model, and whether the board text was supplied at all.

None of the four comparisons is significant. Counted by board, the two speech conditions
split 7 to 6 with board text and 10 to 9 without it, which is as close to a coin toss as
this data can produce. The plus 10.6 points in the item totals is worth taking apart,
because quoting it as the benefit of fine-tuning would be wrong. Of the 37 items gained,
two boards of one lecture give 50 between them, and across the other 31 boards the
fine-tuned transcript is 13 items worse.

The reason the better transcript loses is mechanical. Scoring the two transcripts directly
against the same records, before any notes are written, the off-the-shelf transcript with
67.7 per cent character error contains 106 of the 349 board items and the fine-tuned
transcript with 15.8 per cent contains 91, better on 1 board and worse on 9 with a sign
test at p = 0.021. Off-the-shelf Whisper translates into English, and the records are
largely English technical strings such as NAND gate, MySQL and lines of SQL, so its output
is unusable as a transcript while still emitting those exact strings. The fine-tuned model
writes what the lecturer said, in Banglish, and \textit{amra NAND gate dekhbo} does not
string-match an English item. This is the same trap as the term measure discussed in
Section 5.4.2.

The null result says that the fine-tuned transcript changes almost nothing that
board-content recall can see, and that measure asks only whether facts written on the
board reach the notes. It does not say the transcript is irrelevant to note quality. The
measure is blind by construction to whether the explanation around those facts is correct,
readable or even in the right language, and the two transcripts differ enormously as
transcripts. An English page built from a transcript with 67 per cent character error will
contain sentences that are simply wrong, and nothing here measures that. Testing it
properly needs readers, which is why the reader study is the first item of future work.

The two routes to the English note bear on the same question, and their comparison shows
the same measurement effect from the other side. Writing the English note directly from
the board and the transcript gives 55.9 per cent
recall, while writing the section in Banglish first and translating it gives 89.4, almost
exactly the Banglish original's 89.1. That supports the reading above, in which the
deficit of the direct route is the model paraphrasing the board's strings into English
prose rather than the note carrying less of the lecture.

The caveat is large and is stated with the number every time. Of the 130 items gained, 113
come from four dense boards carrying 39 to 42 items each, and across the other 31 boards
the two routes are level. Counted by board the comparison is 10 better, 7 worse and 18
tied, with a sign test at p = 0.63, and there are real regressions, with two boards falling
from 6 of 7 items to 2 of 7. The translated route is therefore much better on dense,
number-heavy boards and indistinguishable from the direct route on ordinary ones.

\section{Final Design Adjustments}
\label{sec:adjustments}

Evaluation changed the design in several places. What follows is what changed, what
evidence forced it, and what is still wrong.

\subsection{Changes made in response to a measurement}

The loop safeguard was adopted after the small model fell into repetition loops on 58 of
184 clips under plain greedy decoding, and it was applied identically to both models. With
the large model the fine-tuned system loops on one clip in 177, so the safeguard changes
nothing and the reported figures are the plain ones. Both facts are reported, including
that the safeguard was adopted after the greedy result had been seen.

The person mask was replaced. The original took whatever differed from the usual
appearance of the board, which failed when the lecturer stood still and again when the
camera's automatic exposure darkened a frame. A pretrained segmentation network with a
shadow mask replaced it and recovers writing the old mask lost. Frames are also extracted
more densely for a lecturer who does not move, as measured in Section
\ref{sec:board-coverage}.

Each language version is now written wholly in its own language. The first design followed
a mock-up in which an English note quoted the lecturer in Banglish with a translation
beside it, and reading the first real notes showed that an English-medium reader cannot
use a Banglish quotation. The consequence for what can be claimed is stated rather than
glossed over. The word-for-word check runs on the Banglish notes and cannot run on the
English ones. The English note is produced by translating the Banglish note, selected on
the measurement in Section \ref{sec:2x2} with the caveat about the four dense boards.

Four defects found in the real runs are handled in code and are listed here because each
one changes what the output contains. A loop guard collapses repeated identical lines in
the board reader, after one board produced the same line 512 times for 42,037 characters,
which matters because that text is pasted into the note prompt. A salvage parser recovers
the boxes of a board whose code content made the model return broken JSON, which would
otherwise lose every box on that board. A placeholder strip removes the prompt's own
example text when the model copies it into the notes as though it were a quotation, a
failure the quote checker could not catch because it looks for quotations absent from the
transcript and this is not a quotation at all. A label repair maps the section labels the
translation left in Banglish and the summary lines it returned in capital letters. The
page builder also normalises the label on the Background box at render time, after the
model wrote that heading four different ways and 11 of 21 boxes in one rebuild carried no
disclaimer, which defeats the purpose of the box.

\subsection{What is still wrong}
\label{sec:known-defects}

Four defects are known, counted and not fixed. The first is that the box finder treats
board furniture as content. One room's whiteboard carries a
manufacturer's sticker, and because it is dark and separate the box finder puts a box
around it on every board of that lecture. It is cosmetic, costs nothing in any
measurement, and would be fixed by ignoring a region that appears unchanged across every
era of a lecture.

A second defect is that some quotations cannot be translated and are dropped. On the thirteen scored lectures the
translation route kept 15 quotations and dropped 19, and over all 42 lectures that have
notes it kept 60 and dropped 49. A dropped quotation costs the page a lecturer's voice. It
is counted in the output file of every page rather than being silent, and it is the
largest remaining defect in the English version.

A third is that a few references to boxes that do not exist survive. Across the 13 scored Banglish files
the box reference check removed all but one, and over all 42 lectures ten survive in each
language.

The last is that four era boundaries fall in the middle of an erase, as described in
Section
\ref{sec:board-coverage}. The fix is to hold the era open until the wipe finishes.

One further defect is not a software fault and cannot be fixed by a checker. In the notes
for one digital logic lecture the model wrote that there are two types of universal gates,
which are XOR gates, where the lecturer and the board both say NAND and NOR.
Board-content recall cannot catch this, because it measures whether board items appear in
the notes and not whether the prose around them is true. Nothing in this thesis measures
the factual correctness of the generated explanation; the planned reader study is what
would, and an example of the error is more useful to a reader than a claim that it does
not happen.

\section{Statistical Analysis}

\subsection{Tests, medians and the direction of a difference}

Every comparison is paired, because both systems are run on exactly the same clips or
boards, and two tests are applied to each: the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which ranks the
differences by size \cite{wilcoxon1945}, and the exact sign test, which counts only how
many items improved. Both are reported wherever they disagree, as in the two-by-two of
Section \ref{sec:2x2}, where one comparison sits at Wilcoxon p = 0.052 and sign p = 0.18
and neither is treated as significant.

Two reporting rules were adopted after being caught out by their absence. Medians are
reported because both models sometimes fall into a repetition loop and emit far more words
than were spoken, which pushes a mean error rate above 100 per cent; on one early
comparison the same data gave p = 0.76 on means and p = 2.19e-05 on medians. The direction
of the difference is reported because a two sided p-value does not say which system won:
one early adapter was significantly worse than its baseline at z = -2.62 and p = 8.7e-03,
and reporting only the p-value would have made that look like a success.

Recall over record items is dominated by dense boards, because a board with 42 items
contributes fourteen times as much as one with 3, and two results here look very different
depending on which is counted. The translated English route gains 130 items over the
direct route, of which 113 come from four boards, and counted by board it is 10 better and
7 worse at p = 0.63. The two-by-two gains 37 items from fine-tuning, of which 50 come from
two boards while the other 31 lose 13, and counted by board it is 7 better and 6 worse at
p = 1.0. Both countings are reported wherever they differ and the by-board count is the
one the conclusion rests on.

No correction for multiple comparisons has been applied, which is a limitation rather than
an oversight. It matters least for the speech result, where the p-values would survive any
correction, and most for the marginal results, which is why every marginal result here is
described as a trend or as not significant.

\subsection{A metric that moves the wrong way}

The measure inherited from the earlier phase of this project is a term based F1 over a
fixed list of 82 English words. Two independent training runs on identical data, with the
same recipe and seed and differing only in the order the manifest listed the clips, gave
the results in Table \ref{tab:termf1}.

The speech result replicates, with both runs beating the base model at p below 0.01 on
word and character error rate. Term F1 does not, swinging 8.1 points on identical data,
driven by an 11.4 point swing in term precision. Term precision counts how many of the 82
fixed English words the model emits, and run B transcribes better Banglish, so it emits
fewer English words and its term precision falls. The measure penalises the model for
doing the task correctly.

Three consequences follow and are applied throughout this thesis. A term F1 difference
smaller than about 8 points is never claimed, which independently rules out the fusion
result of 0.7 points. Word and character error rate with their rank tests are the
headline. Both runs are reported rather than the better one being quietly chosen.

The same effect reappears in the note evaluation of Section \ref{sec:2x2}, where the worse
transcript contains more of the English record items. Two independent instances of the
same failure in one project is why this thesis treats it as a finding rather than a
nuisance, and it agrees with the published work on evaluation metrics for code-switched
recognition discussed in Section 2.2.3.

\section{Comparisons and Relationships}

\subsection{Comparison with existing systems}

Table \ref{tab:neighbours} in Chapter 2 places this work beside the four nearest
Bengali-English code-switched speech systems. The comparison cannot be made on the
numbers, because two of the four are trained and evaluated on synthetic speech, one
targets the Bengali script and one is an undocumented public model evaluated on a random
split of its own clips. What can be compared is the design of the evaluation, and there
this work is stricter in three ways. Whole lectures are held out, so no part of a test
recording appears in training; the references are checked word by word by a person rather
than generated; and the same system is reported under two designs, one with the test
lecturers heard in training and one with an entirely unseen lecturer.

For the board side the comparison is different in kind. Systems such as those of Davila
and Zanibbi \cite{davila2017whiteboard} and Kota et al. \cite{kota2019generalized} extract
and binarise handwriting from fixed camera lecture video and report component level recall
above 94 per cent on public datasets. This work does not beat them at that task and does
not attempt to, since its reconstruction is engineering assembled from known parts. What
is added is the step after it. Reading the rebuilt board into text with a vision-language
model, scoring that against hand-verified records, and carrying numbered regions of the
board into a generated note.

\subsection{Relationships between data, stages and the earlier phase}

Two observations connect the amount of data to the result and neither is a controlled
experiment. Within the final result the lecturer with the least training speech has by far
the worst test lecture, at 52 per cent character error against 12 to 16 for the others.
Across designs, holding out an entire lecturer and training on 80 to 114 minutes gives
50.3 per cent character error, while holding out whole lectures from known lecturers and
training on 4.10 hours gives 15.8. The two differ in both the amount of data and the
difficulty of the question, so neither isolates the effect of data volume. An earlier
scaling curve on the smaller model, at 0.30, 0.60 and 0.93 hours, gave 84.6, 80.6 and 83.3
per cent word error, which is flat within the 4.5 point run-to-run noise after the first
18 minutes. A curve on the current corpus was planned and cancelled when the corpus
closed.

The clearest relationship between the stages is that the factual content of a note comes
from the vision side rather than the speech side. Adding the board text moves note recall
by 18.3 points with the fine-tuned transcript and 4.9 points with the off-the-shelf one,
while changing the transcript with board text present moves nothing a by-board test can
detect. This is a statement about what board-content recall measures rather than a defect
of the speech work, and it is the reason the speech result is reported on its own terms as
a transcription result.

The earlier phase of the project reported a fusion improvement of 5.7 percentage points
with p = 0.003 and a large effect size, an ablation table, a bias sweep and a set of
per-video statistics, none of which was produced by any code in the project. They were
re-derived where a derivation was possible and retired where it was not. The measured
fusion effect is 0.7 points at p = 0.32, two of the ablation rows described modes the
pipeline does not have, and a failure-mode breakdown of five neat percentages summing to
100 had no analysis behind it and was removed rather than reconstructed. This is recorded
because it is the reason for the project rule that a number needs a command, and because a
reader comparing this report with the earlier one is entitled to know which numbers
changed and why.

\section{Discussions}

\subsection{What the results mean}

The speech result is the strongest part of the thesis. A small adapter on a large
multilingual model, trained on 4.10 hours of hand-checked code-mixed classroom speech,
cuts the character error rate from 67.7 to about 16 per cent on lectures the model has
never heard, with two seeds agreeing and almost every clip improving. In practical terms
the output changes from something a transcriber cannot use into a draft a transcriber can
correct.

The board result is a result about prompting rather than about model capability, since the
same model on the same images moves from 31.2 to 88.8 per cent recall purely on how it is
asked, and a model less than half the size reaches 85.4 per cent. Anyone building a system
of this kind should spend effort on the prompt before spending it on a bigger model. The
note result is that board content can be made to reach the page. The annotated notes carry
89.1 per cent of the board items against 37.2 for a conventional summarisation prompt,
while remaining a readable document rather than a paste of extracted text.

The negative results matter as much. Fusion, decoder bias and occlusion as a pointer were
all built and all measured, and none worked; the display clean-up looks better and reads
slightly worse; and fine-tuning the speech model does not move note recall. Reporting
these is what makes the positive results credible.

\subsection{Implications}

For code-mixed speech, the data is the bottleneck and the method is not exotic. A standard
adapter on a standard model gives a large gain, and what took the effort was five hours of
carefully checked transcription and a split design that does not leak.

For evaluation, measures assembled out of English strings systematically reward a system
that translates code-mixed speech and punish one that transcribes it. It happened twice
here in two unrelated measures, and it is consistent with the published work on
code-switched evaluation reviewed in Section 2.2.3. Anyone evaluating a code-mixed system
on an English-derived metric should check for this before believing the result.

For classroom systems, the whiteboard is worth the trouble. Most of the facts a student
needs from these lectures are on the board rather than in the speech, and a pipeline that
reads the board recovers them.

\subsection{Limitations and threats to validity}

The limitations are listed together rather than spread through the chapter.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item No human evaluation of the notes. The measure counts whether board content reached
        the page and cannot see whether the explanation is correct, readable or useful, and
        a factual error in the prose has already been found by reading.
  \item Three lecturers, one university, one language pair. Nothing here shows that the
        result transfers to another institution, subject area or code-mixed pair.
  \item 5.15 hours of transcribed speech, which is small by the standards of speech
        recognition, and the relationship between hours and accuracy at this scale is not
        measured.
  \item Training is unstable at the selected learning rate, since one seed of the
        pre-registered configuration diverged completely, and the stable configuration
        reported beside it was chosen after that was seen.
  \item The board-reading prompt was chosen on the boards it is reported on. A held-out
        half was checked afterwards and supports the conclusion without undoing the
        selection.
  \item Box finding is not evaluated, because no layout ground truth exists. It works well
        on sparse boards and coarsely on dense ones, and it draws a box around a sticker.
  \item Quotations in the English notes are not verified, since they are translations and
        the automatic check cannot apply to them.
  \item No comparison against a neighbouring model on our own data. Running a public
        Bengali-English model on our test clips would turn a discussion into a table, and
        it was not done before submission.
  \item No correction for multiple comparisons, which is why marginal results are described
        as trends.
  \item The system is for recorded lectures and nothing here runs in real time.
\end{enumerate}

The main threat to the speech result would be leakage between training and test. Whole
lecture splits, the voice check on every recording and the frozen record of which lectures
were on each side of every split guard against it. One leak did occur earlier in the
project, it was found by the voice check, and the affected claim was retired.

The main threat to the vision results is that the board records were drafted by reading
the rebuilt boards, which could favour the reconstruction over a raw frame. The records
were then checked by a person against the board images without seeing any model output,
and the comparison between the reconstruction and the raw frame is reported as a trend
rather than a result, so the conclusion does not depend on it.

The main threat to the note results is that board-content recall is a narrow measure that
a system could satisfy by pasting the board text into the page. That is exactly what the
variant at 88.0 per cent does, which is why the annotated notes are compared against it
rather than only against the original pipeline.
