\section{Design Process or Methodology Overview}

\subsection{Design objectives and the shape of the solution}

The design follows from the objectives in Section 1.4 and the constraints in Section
3.1.3. A lecture video is the input and a lecture note containing the lecturer's words
and the lecturer's board is the output. The system must run on one desktop graphics card,
it must be measurable stage by stage, and it must not invent content.

One option was a single multimodal model that reads the video and writes the note. It was
rejected for three reasons. A single model gives one number at the end, so when that
number is poor there is nothing to inspect, whereas a pipeline gives a separate
measurement for the speech, the board reconstruction, the board reading and the note.
Chapter 5 shows this paying off, because the four stages turned out to contribute very
differently. Keeping the stages apart also means only one model is in memory at a time,
which matters on the hardware available. Finally, when the board is read into text as a
separate step the note-writing model receives the board as text instead of having to
remember it, so a fact in the note can be traced to the board. When one model both looks
at the image and writes the note, a fact cannot be traced to its source.

Four rules were fixed before building and were not relaxed. Each stage is a separate
program with a file interface, and a stage is skipped when its output already exists, so
an interrupted run continues and a single stage can be replaced without touching the
others. Nothing in the image is invented. The board reconstruction may leave a visible
gap but may not fill one with plausible pixels. The board records used for scoring sit in
a folder that the note generator refuses to read, enforced in code rather than by
discipline. And every reported number is produced by a command recorded beside it.

\subsection{The four stages}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.97\textwidth]{fig-4-1-pipeline}
\caption{The InsightLens pipeline. Each stage writes a file that
the next stage reads.}
\label{fig:fig-4-1-pipeline}

The first two stages work on the two halves of the recording independently. In stage one
the audio is extracted and cut into clips, a LoRA adapter trained on the corpus is
attached to Whisper large-v3-turbo, and the adapted model writes a timestamped transcript
in romanized Banglish. In stage two frames are extracted at a fixed interval, erase events
split the lecture into eras with one board per era, and within an era each tile of the
frame is taken from a moment when the lecturer was not standing in front of that tile.

The last two stages turn the board into text and then combine the two halves. In stage
three the blocks of writing are found on the clean board, a numbered coloured box is drawn
around each, and Qwen2.5-VL names and transcribes each numbered box. In stage four a
language model receives each board's boxes, the board text and the part of the transcript
belonging to that board, and returns one section of notes that points at the boxes.
Checkers then remove unverifiable quotations and bad box references, and the sections are
assembled into a page.

\section{Preliminary Design and Alternatives Considered}

Several designs were built and measured before the final one was chosen. Three of them
failed, and the failures are part of the argument for the design that was kept. Table
\ref{tab:alternatives} summarises the comparison, and the paragraphs below give the
reasoning where the table cannot.

For the speech stage the two rejected-by-measurement options are the informative ones.
Running an English recogniser and a Bengali recogniser and merging their outputs was built
and measured on nine lectures. The merged output scored 0.7 percentage points above the
baseline on the term measure, which a paired permutation test puts at p = 0.32, while its
word error rate was far worse at 146.8 per cent against 82.6. Biasing the decoder towards
words seen on the board, implemented as a logits processor with a strength parameter,
halved term recall at every non-zero setting and truncated the transcript. Using a Bengali
model and transliterating afterwards was rejected on the design and not by experiment,
because it loses the English technical terms twice, first when the Bengali model spells
them phonetically and again when they are romanized back.

For the board, the option that had to be abandoned was the one the first version of the
project used, which removed the lecturer and filled the hole by inpainting. Inspection
against the source frame found the lecturer still visible as a grey silhouette, because
the brightness threshold missed the light squares of his shirt, and text he was writing at
that moment lost, because the filled region was rebuilt from a single donor frame. The
filled figure also showed content not yet written at that time, so it was a plausible
picture of a board that never existed. Generative inpainting with a diffusion model was
not attempted, because on a whiteboard a plausible fill is invented writing and a reader
cannot distinguish it from the real thing. The tiled mosaic over erase separated eras was
selected because every output pixel is unmodified camera output, so the only question is
whether each tile found a clear moment, and that is counted exactly.

For reading the board and for writing the note, the choices were settled by the
measurements reported in Chapter 5 and are summarised in the table. One option remains
untested. The code supports asking the vision-language model to draw the boxes itself, but
our own box finder was kept because it is deterministic and its output can be drawn onto
the real image and checked by eye.

\section{Data Collection}

\subsection{Recording and transcription}

Forty-four lectures were recorded in Brac University classrooms with a fixed camera
pointed at the lecturer and the whiteboard. Three lecturers took part and no lecture uses
slides. The recordings total 8.33 hours, the shortest 3.4 minutes and the longest 36.4
minutes.

The lecturer of each recording was confirmed by voice, not by memory. A WavLM
speaker embedding was computed for every lecture and compared with reference embeddings,
giving cosine similarity between 0.98 and 0.998 within a lecturer and between 0.59 and
0.90 across lecturers. This check exists because an early version of the split
attributed one lecture to the wrong lecturer, which made a result look better than it
was; the error is described in Section \ref{sec:asr-loso}.

Before transcription began, a written standard was produced, and every transcript is
checked against it by a validation script. Each line is a timed segment written as
\texttt{[M:SS-M:SS]} followed by the text. Segments run 10 to 25 seconds and never longer
than 30, because Whisper processes a 30 second window, and there are no gaps and no
overlaps between them. Fillers such as \textit{aaa}, \textit{mane} and \textit{tahole} are
kept, because the goal is a transcript of the speech and not a tidied version of it.
The text is ASCII only, and a table of canonical spellings fixes the most common words so
that three transcribers do not write the same word three ways. Appendix A gives the
standard in full. Transcription was done by hand, with every
word checked against the audio by a member of the team, at a cost of about one hour of
work for ten minutes of video.

\subsection{Data Cleaning}

Four cleaning problems occurred and each is handled by a script and not by hand editing. The first concerns timestamps. The segment parser accepts a timestamp only in
the exact form \texttt{[1:34-1:53]}. In an
earlier version of the corpus, 9 of 156 timestamps carried stray spaces, written for
example as \texttt{[4:22- 4:45 ]}. Those lines did not register as segment boundaries, so
their text joined the previous segment and ten evaluation clips carried more words than
their audio. The validator now reports every unreadable timestamp and can repair them,
keeping a backup of the original file. The effect of the defect on the affected result
was measured rather than assumed and is reported in Section \ref{sec:asr-loso}.

The second is incomplete files. A transcript still being written is marked by its file
name and skipped until the marker
is removed. Files were also delivered at one point with a placeholder extension that made
them invisible to the pipeline, which was found by counting how many transcripts the
loader actually saw instead of how many files existed.

The third is missing lecturer labels. Where the header was absent it was filled from
the voice check rather than
guessed, and any recording where the voice check was not confident was left for a person
to decide. A preflight script then refuses to start training if a transcript has no matching video, if two
different videos share a name, if a transcript is longer than its video, or if a lecturer
label disagrees with the voice check. It runs as a hard gate before every training run,
because all of these faults corrupt training silently instead of crashing.

\subsection{Data Transformation and Integration}

Each video is converted to 16 kHz mono PCM WAV, the input format Whisper expects. The
human transcript gives the segment boundaries, so one clip is cut per segment, and
segments longer than the model's 30 second window are subdivided at sentence ends. Each
clip becomes one training example of an audio array and its reference text. The audio is
turned into a log-Mel spectrogram by the model's own feature extractor and the reference
text is tokenised by the model's own tokeniser, so no custom preprocessing can differ
between training and evaluation. For the vision side, frames are extracted at a fixed
interval and scaled so that the longer side is at most 1920 pixels. A 10 second interval
suffices for lecturers who move about, while a 2 second interval gives each tile five
times as many chances to see the board clear for a lecturer who stands still.

The corpus was renumbered part way through the project so that lectures are grouped by
lecturer. Because every result produced before the renumbering used the old names, a
lookup table records the correspondence, and every script that finds a lecture's audio or
board uses the table instead of assuming the number in a file name means the same thing
everywhere. The ground truth behind the earlier results is frozen in a separate folder, so
those results can still be reproduced. Three sources have to line up for one lecture,
which are the video, the transcript and the rebuilt boards; they are joined by lecture
number through that table, and the preflight script checks the join before any training.

No dimensionality reduction is applied to the audio or the images, since the models
consume raw signals. Reduction happens in three other places. Lectures without a
transcript take no part in training or in the speech evaluation, which leaves 28 of the 44
for the speech experiments, while all 44 are available to the vision side. For scoring the
notes only the 13 lectures with hand-verified board records can be used; the other 30
receive the same notes as demonstrations and are not scored, and the report says which is
which. Within the board stage an era shorter than four frames is ignored, because too few
frames means too few chances for a tile to see the board clear.

\subsection{The splits}

Three separate splits are used and they must not be confused. The validation split
chooses settings. Three lectures, one per lecturer, were drawn with a
fixed seed before any tuning began and recorded in a file that the data preparation script
reads. They are BanglaASR2 from lecturer A, BanglaASR12 from B and BanglaASR14 from C,
34.2 minutes in total, used only to score tuning runs and locked out of every test set
afterwards.

The test split gives the final result. Whole lectures are held out at random, about 20 per
cent of each lecturer's minutes, with the seed fixed at zero and the rule written down
before the draw. A lecture is never cut between training and test and every lecturer
appears on both sides. The draw selected BanglaASR8, 9, 11, 15, 19 and 27, which is 1.05
hours and 177 clips, leaving 4.10 hours and 22 lectures for training. Because the test
lecturers are also heard in training, this design answers the question of new lectures
from known lecturers, and the report labels it that way whenever it is quoted.

The leave-one-speaker-out splits answer the harder question. Each lecturer in turn is held
out entirely and the model is trained on the other two, which gives worse numbers, and
both designs are reported.

One further rule protects the note evaluation. A note scored on a lecture's board records
must be written from a transcript produced by an adapter that never heard that lecture's
lecturer, so the transcripts for the scored lectures come from the leave-one-speaker-out
adapters, and the builder records in each output file which adapter produced its input.

\section{Implementation of Selected Design}

\subsection{Adapting the speech model}

The base model is whisper-large-v3-turbo, an encoder-decoder transformer with 809 million
parameters \cite{radford2023whisper}, and its weights are frozen. LoRA adapters are
attached to the query and value projection matrices of the attention layers, with rank 16
and scaling factor 32 \cite{hu2022lora}. Training uses a batch size of 8, 8 epochs,
bfloat16 precision and gradient checkpointing on the 12 GB card, which reduces the memory
needed from about 18 GB to 6.1 GB at the cost of recomputing activations. Every run is
done with two random seeds and both are reported.

The reported numbers use plain greedy decoding. The loop safeguard from the Whisper paper
is also available and is reported beside the main result. A hypothesis whose gzip
compression ratio exceeds 2.4 is decoded again with sampling at rising temperatures until
the output stops looping. It reads only the hypothesis, never the reference, and is
applied identically to the base and the adapted model.

The settings come from a plan committed to version control before any tuning run, which
fixes the validation lectures, the order of the search and the rule for accepting a
winner. The plan has five stages, run in order, each starting from the winner of the
previous one.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Learning rate, over 5e-4, 1e-3 and 2e-3.
  \item LoRA rank, over 8, 16 and 32, with the scaling factor at twice the rank.
  \item The adapted layers, comparing the query and value projections against all four
        attention projections.
  \item The number of epochs, taken from the validation loss curve.
  \item Seed stability, by repeating the winner with a second seed.
\end{enumerate}

Everything else is held fixed. The score is the median per-clip character error rate on
the validation clips, and a candidate replaces the default only if it beats the default by
more than the spread between two seeds of the same configuration, which is the plan's way
of refusing to chase noise. The procedure was run twice, first as a rehearsal on 2.69
hours of transcript to check that the machinery worked, then on the full corpus once
transcription closed; the second run supersedes the first and Section \ref{sec:asr-tuning}
reports it.

\subsection{Rebuilding the board}

The amount of ink on the board is tracked across frames, and when a large fraction of it
disappears between consecutive frames the board has been wiped, so the frames between two
wipes form one era. Within an era the frame is cut into small overlapping tiles. A tile
counts as clear in a given frame when almost none of it is covered by the lecturer, and
for each tile independently the pipeline takes the latest frame in which that tile was
clear. The chosen tiles are blended back together with a raised-cosine weight so that the
seams do not show.

The method works because the lecturer occupies one place at a time, which is also its
stated limit. It needs the lecturer to move. Where a tile never finds a clear moment the
residue is left visible and reported instead of filled in.

Locating the lecturer has three implementations and the third is used. The simplest takes
whatever differs from the usual appearance of the board, which fails when the lecturer
stands still, because then he is part of the usual appearance, and fails again when the
camera's automatic exposure darkens a frame, because then every pixel differs. The second
uses a pretrained DeepLabV3 network with a ResNet-101 backbone and takes its person class
frame by frame \cite{chen2017deeplabv3}. The third adds a shadow mask, computed as board
darker than usual after dividing out each frame's exposure gain, with pen-stroke-thin
marks eroded away so that writing is not mistaken for shadow. The third recovers writing
the first lost, including a diagram drawn at the end of an era and seven of the eight
digits of a binary number that the old mask reduced to four.

A separate clean-up step flattens the background and fades everything not clearly darker
than its surroundings to white, in the manner of the whiteboard mode of a document
scanner, and blanks the pixels the mosaic flagged as the lecturer. Because it replaces
background pixels, a cleaned board is not unmodified camera output. The strokes are camera
pixels and the background is not, and the report says so wherever a cleaned board is
shown. Section \ref{sec:board-cleanup} reports the measured effect of this step on how
well the vision-language model reads the board, which was not the expected one.

\subsection{Numbered boxes and the vision-language model}

Our own code, not the model, finds the blocks of writing. It groups dark pixels, strips
the board frame, drops specks and rejoins boxes that are parts of the same written line,
producing at most ten boxes per board. Each box receives a number and a colour from a
fixed list of ten, and the notes later refer to a box by both, for example the purple box
5.

The numbered boxes are drawn onto the clean board and the image is given to
Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct with a prompt asking, for each numbered box, for a short name and a
full transcription of what is inside it. This is Set-of-Mark prompting \cite{yang2023som}
with the marks supplied by our code. Boxes the model reports as empty are dropped.

Two defects were found during the real runs and both are handled in code. On one board the
model read the content correctly, then tried to draw a diagram's diagonal and emitted the
same backslash line 512 times, giving 42,037 characters; the reader now collapses runs of
identical lines and records how many it dropped, which matters because that text is pasted
into the note prompt. Separately, boards carrying code make the model emit unescaped
quotation marks that break the JSON it is asked to return, and without a fallback such a
board loses all of its boxes, so a salvage parser reads the fields one at a time when the
JSON cannot be parsed.

\subsection{Writing the note}

The note is written by Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, one call per board. Each call receives the
board's boxes with their names and transcribed text, and the part of the transcript
falling between that board's first and last frame, so the model sees the words spoken
while that board was on the wall. A final call produces the title, the takeaways and the
self-check questions for the whole lecture.

The prompt asks for a section that walks the reader through the board, refers to the boxes
by number and colour, and quotes the lecturer between two and five times. Material that is
standard for the topic but that the lecturer did not say is allowed only inside a section
headed \texttt{Background}, which the page builder renders as a dashed, labelled aside. This
is the controlled version of the failure that produced the wrong textbook definition in
the first version of the pipeline. The material is still generated, but it is fenced and
labelled rather than passing as something the lecturer said.

Three checks then run over the generated text. The quote checker searches the transcript
for every block quotation and deletes any that is not there word for word, writing the
count of kept and deleted quotations into the output file. A second counter flags quoted
spans of four or more words inside ordinary paragraphs that match neither the transcript
nor the board, but only flags them, since most turn out to be code or translated lines
rather than errors. The box reference check removes any reference to a box number the
board does not have and counts them. The placeholder strip removes the prompt's own
example text when the model copies it into the notes instead of leaving the quotation out,
a failure found by reading the output.

Each language version is written wholly in its own language. The Banglish note quotes the
lecturer's real words, so the quote checker applies to it. The English note quotes the
lecturer in English translation, and a translation cannot be matched against a Banglish
transcript, so the checker does not run on the English files. Every output file records
which of the two it is, and Chapter 5 states the resulting claim precisely. The English
note is produced by writing the section in Banglish first and then translating it with the
same model, keeping the Markdown structure, the tables, the box numbers and the colours. A
defect in this route was found and fixed. The translation left 97 section labels in
Banglish across the thirteen scored files and returned three summary lines in capital
letters, both repaired by a mapping script that does not call the model, since the labels
are a fixed set written by our own prompt.

The sections are finally assembled into Markdown and into one self-contained HTML page
with the board images embedded, coloured tags for the box references and click-to-reveal
answers for the self-check questions.

\subsection{Running the system, and how it is evaluated}

The whole pipeline is one command that takes a video and produces the notes. It runs each
stage as a separate process, skips any stage whose output already exists, and writes the
time each stage took to a file beside the outputs, which is what makes the run times in
Section 3.3 measurements rather than estimates.

Speech is evaluated by computing word and character error rate for each test clip
separately and reporting the median, because a single runaway output can push a mean above
100 per cent. The base model and the adapted model are run on exactly the same clips, so
the comparison is paired and is tested with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test and a sign test,
and the number of runaway clips is reported for both. Two spelling tolerant variants are
also computed, with the rules committed before any rescoring. The first maps both sides to
the transcription guide's canonical spellings and collapses repeated vowels, and the
second additionally counts a word as correct at 80 per cent character similarity, so words
of three letters or fewer must still match exactly.

For the board reconstruction two different things are counted and must not be confused.
The fraction of tiles that found a completely clear view is computed by the reconstruction
itself and measures coverage and not whether writing survived. Whether writing
survived was answered by a person, who viewed each rebuilt board beside two real frames
from the same time and said whether anything written was missing and what it was.

Board reading and note quality are both scored by recall of items from the hand-verified
records. A record is a list of the things written on one board, each labelled as a number,
name, code fragment, term or phrase. Recall is the right measure and not F1, because
good notes legitimately contain a great deal that was not on the board, so precision would
punish the behaviour we want. Items of three characters or fewer must match as whole
words, so that \textit{CS} is not found inside \textit{economics}. The records were
drafted from the board images and then checked by hand, one board at a time, without the
checker seeing any model output, and the folder holding them is refused by the note
generator in code.

Three reporting rules apply throughout Chapter 5. Medians are reported, not means.
The direction of every difference is stated beside its p-value. Counting by board is
reported alongside counting by item, because the item count is dominated by a few dense
boards and the two can disagree.
