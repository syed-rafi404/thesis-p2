\section{Preliminaries}

This section explains the ideas and the vocabulary that the rest of the thesis
uses. A reader who already knows speech recognition and vision-language models
can move to Section 2.2.

\subsection{Code-mixing, code-switching and Banglish}

Code-switching is the use of more than one language by the same speaker in the
same conversation. Linguists usually separate two cases. Inter-sentential
switching means the speaker finishes a sentence in one language and starts the
next in another. Intra-sentential switching, also called code-mixing, means the
switch happens inside a single sentence. The second case is the harder one for
speech systems, because an utterance cannot be assigned to one language and
handed to one monolingual model \cite{agro2025cssurvey}.

Bangladeshi classroom speech is strongly intra-sentential. In the recordings used
in this thesis a single clause regularly carries a Bengali verb, an English noun
phrase and an English technical term. The speech is Bengali in grammar and often
English in content words.

The written form matters as much as the spoken one. Bengali has its own script,
but Bangladeshi students very often write Bengali in the Roman alphabet, a form
usually called Banglish. There is no spelling standard for it, so the same word
appears as \textit{ami}, \textit{aami} or \textit{amee} depending on the writer.
Romanized Bangla is now a recognised object of study in natural language
processing. BanglaTLit, for example, is a benchmark of 42.7 thousand romanized
Bangla samples with a pre-training corpus of 245.7 thousand more, built for
converting romanized Bangla back into the Bengali script
\cite{fahim2024banglatlit}. Work of this kind establishes that romanized Bengali
is a real written form with real users, which is the reason this thesis chose it
as the output of the speech model rather than the Bengali script.

\subsection{Automatic speech recognition and how its errors are measured}

Automatic speech recognition, or ASR, takes an audio signal and returns text. The
standard measure of its quality is the word error rate, which is the number of
word substitutions, insertions and deletions needed to turn the output into the
reference, divided by the number of words in the reference. The character error
rate is the same calculation on characters. Both are error measures, so lower is
better, and both can go above 100 per cent if the system produces more text than
was spoken.

Two practical points about these measures matter for this thesis. First, a single
model can produce a very long wrong output for one short clip, which pushes a
mean error rate above 100 per cent and hides everything else. Reporting the median
error over clips avoids that, and the paired tests described below compare clips
one by one instead of averaging them. Second, word error rate is unkind to a
language with no spelling standard, because two acceptable spellings of the same
word count as an error. Section 2.2.4 reviews what the code-switching literature
does about this, and Chapter 4 describes the spelling-tolerant variant that was
computed here.

\subsection{Whisper and encoder-decoder speech models}

Whisper is a family of encoder-decoder transformer models trained on about 680
thousand hours of weakly supervised multilingual audio \cite{radford2023whisper}.
The audio is turned into a log-Mel spectrogram, the encoder produces a sequence of
hidden states from it, and the decoder generates text tokens one at a time while
attending to the encoder output. The decoder is also given special tokens that
state the language and the task, where the task is either transcribe or translate.

This design explains two behaviours that shaped the present work. Because the
model is told which single language it is hearing, mixed-language audio is pushed
towards one of them, and for Bengali audio containing English words the model very
often produces English. Because the decoder is autoregressive and has a strong
internal language model, it can also enter a repetition loop where the same phrase
is emitted until the token limit is reached. This behaviour is well documented.
The Whisper paper itself proposes a fallback rule for it, in which a hypothesis
whose gzip compression ratio is above a threshold is decoded again with sampling
\cite{radford2023whisper}, and later work such as WhisperX avoids the problem by
segmenting audio with an external voice activity detector instead of relying on
the model's own timestamps \cite{bain2023whisperx}. Loops are reported in this
thesis as a counted quantity rather than being quietly removed.

\subsection{Fine-tuning, and Low-Rank Adaptation}

Fine-tuning means continuing to train a pretrained model on new data so that it
fits a new domain. Training all the weights of a model with hundreds of millions
of parameters needs a large amount of memory, because the optimiser keeps extra
state for every parameter, and it also risks destroying what the model already
knows when the new dataset is small.

Low-Rank Adaptation, or LoRA, avoids both problems \cite{hu2022lora}. The original
weight matrix is frozen. Beside it, two small matrices are trained whose product
has the same shape as the original, and the output of the layer becomes the sum of
the frozen path and the small trained path. If the original matrix is $d \times k$
and the two small matrices have inner dimension $r$, the number of trained
parameters falls from $d \times k$ to $r(d + k)$, and $r$ is typically 8, 16 or 32.
The frozen model is untouched, so the adapter can be removed or swapped. In speech
work the adapters are usually placed on the attention projections, most often the
query and value projections \cite{yang2025spt}.

Two terms used throughout the thesis follow from this. The \textbf{rank} $r$
controls how much the adapter can change the model. The \textbf{learning rate}
controls how big each training step is. Both are hyperparameters, meaning they are
chosen by the person training the model rather than learned from the data, and
Section 2.1.6 explains how they should be chosen.

\subsection{Validation, testing, ablation and comparison}

Four ideas are used repeatedly in Chapter 4 and Chapter 5, and they are defined
here once.

\textbf{Training, validation and test data.} The training set is what the model
learns from. The validation set is used to choose settings such as the learning
rate. The test set is used once, at the end, to report the result. If settings are
chosen by looking at the test set, the reported number is optimistic, because the
choice has been fitted to the very data that is supposed to be unseen. Keeping the
three sets separate is the single most important protection against reporting a
number that will not hold up.

\textbf{Hyperparameter tuning.} This is the search over settings. It is honest
only if the search is scored on validation data, and it is more honest still if
the order of the search and the rule for accepting a winner are written down
before any run, so that the search cannot quietly turn into a hunt for the best
test number. Chapter 4 describes the pre-registered plan used here.

\textbf{Ablation.} An ablation removes or changes one component of a system and
measures what happens, which is how a claim of the form ``this part is what makes
it work'' is supported. This thesis runs several, including a comparison of two
sizes of vision-language model and a two-by-two study of the speech model against
the board reader.

\textbf{Held-out speakers and held-out lectures.} In speech work it matters
greatly whether the test speaker was heard during training. Testing on unseen
speakers is harder and is the stronger claim. Testing on unseen recordings from
speakers who also appear in training is easier and must be labelled as such. Both
designs are reported separately in this thesis.

\subsection{Statistical testing}

Comparing two systems on a set of clips is a paired problem, because both systems
are run on exactly the same clips. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test ranks the
differences by size and asks whether the positive and negative differences are
balanced \cite{wilcoxon1945}. The sign test is weaker but makes fewer assumptions,
since it only counts how many items improved and how many got worse. Both are used
here, and the direction of the difference is always reported beside the p-value,
because a two-sided p-value on its own does not say which system won.

\subsection{Vision-language models and visual prompting}

A vision-language model takes an image and a text instruction and returns text.
Modern open models such as Qwen2.5-VL use a vision transformer encoder with
native-resolution input and a language model decoder, and they read text inside an
image well enough that a separate optical character recognition stage is often not
needed \cite{bai2025qwen25vl}. That is the property this thesis relies on for
reading handwriting on a whiteboard.

Getting such a model to answer about a specific part of an image is a separate
problem. Set-of-Mark prompting solves it by drawing marks, such as numbered boxes,
directly onto the image and asking the model to answer per mark
\cite{yang2023som}. The marks give the model and the user a shared vocabulary for
positions in the image. The board reader in this thesis uses the same idea, with
the difference that the boxes are found by our own image processing rather than by
a segmentation network, and the numbers then carry through into the generated
notes so that a sentence can say which box it is describing.

The output of these models is also very sensitive to how the instruction is
written. Promptception, a systematic study of prompt sensitivity across ten large
multimodal models, reports accuracy swings of up to 15 per cent from minor changes
in phrasing and structure \cite{ismithdeen2025promptception}. That finding is the
published context for one of this thesis's own results, in which changing the
board-reading prompt was worth far more than changing the size of the model.

\subsection{Language models for note writing, and the faithfulness problem}

An instruction-tuned language model such as Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct can be given a
transcript and asked for structured notes \cite{qwen2025qwen25}. The risk is
well known from the summarisation literature. Models produce fluent text that is
not supported by the input, and a study of state-of-the-art abstractive
summarisers found that a large share of generated summaries contain hallucinated
content \cite{maynez2020faithfulness}. The distinction usually drawn is between
faithfulness, which asks whether the output follows the input given to the model,
and factuality, which asks whether the output is true of the world. A lecture note
must be faithful, because a statement that is true in general but was not part of
the lecture is still the wrong note.

This thesis handles the problem in three ways that are described in Chapter 4. The
board content is passed to the model as text so that it does not have to be
recalled. Material the lecturer did not say is allowed but is confined to a
labelled box. Quotations are checked word for word against the transcript and
deleted if they do not match.

\subsection{Speech recognition for Bengali}

Bengali speech recognition is an active area with real resources behind it.
OOD-Speech is the largest public Bengali corpus of its kind, with 1177.94 hours of
crowdsourced training audio from 22,645 native speakers and a 23.03 hour test set
manually annotated from seventeen different sources, including television drama,
audiobooks, talk shows and online classes \cite{rakib2023oodspeech}. Its purpose
is to measure how badly models degrade when the test domain differs from the
training domain, which is exactly the failure this thesis would meet if it trained
on read speech and tested on a classroom.

On the modelling side, self-supervised encoders and fine-tuned Whisper models both
work for Bengali. Applying wav2vec2 to the Bengali Common Voice dataset gives a
word error rate of 0.2524 on a validation split of 7,747 samples
\cite{shahgir2022wav2vec2bengali}, and a number of Whisper models fine-tuned on
Bengali Common Voice are available publicly. The model used as the Bengali branch
of the earlier phase of this project, \texttt{bangla-speech-processing/BanglaASR},
is one of these, a Whisper-small fine-tuned on Bengali Common Voice.

Every one of these systems writes Bengali script. For a classroom in which the
technical vocabulary is English, that is a problem rather than a solution. The
output turns ``NAND gate'' into a Bengali spelling of the English sound, so a
student searching for the English term will not find it, and a reader who writes
in Banglish cannot match it either. This is the first half of the gap this thesis
addresses.

\subsection{Code-switched speech recognition}

Code-switched ASR has its own literature, surveyed in general terms by Sitaram et
al. \cite{sitaram2019survey} and, for end-to-end systems specifically, in a recent
systematic review that catalogues the languages, datasets, metrics and model
choices used across the field \cite{agro2025cssurvey}. The review makes two
observations that apply directly here. Intra-sentential switching is the hard
case, and the field is short of natural, spontaneous, in-domain speech data,
because most corpora are read or scripted.

For South Asian languages, Chadha et al. built code-switched and code-mixed
recognition for Indic languages using wav2vec 2.0 with language identification,
reporting word error rates of 21.77 for Hindi-English and 28.27 for
Bengali-English \cite{chadha2022indic}. Those numbers are much lower than the ones
reported in this thesis, and the reason is not that their system is better at our
task. Their transcription target is the native script for the Bengali parts, their
audio comes from a different and generally cleaner domain, and their test split is
not made of whole unseen lectures. Error rates across different datasets and
different transcription targets are not comparable, and this thesis does not
present them as if they were.

On the efficiency side, Yang et al. study parameter-efficient adaptation of Whisper
for code-switching, comparing soft prompt tuning variants against LoRA on the SEAME
and ASRU2019 Mandarin-English datasets, and find deep prompt tuning to be
competitive with LoRA while preserving performance on the original languages
\cite{yang2025spt}. Their conclusion supports the design used here, which is that
a small adapter on a frozen multilingual model is an appropriate way to add a
code-switched variety without retraining everything.

Reading the table in order, MediBeng is a translation system, not a transcription
system, and its training data is synthetic \cite{ghosh2025medibeng}. Its reported
word error rate of 0.01 with a BLEU score of 0.98 is what synthetic data produces
and should not be read as accuracy on real clinical speech. BEHE-CMDisfl is
likewise synthetic, generated by prompting a language model for code-mixed text
with disfluencies and then rendering it with a text-to-speech system
\cite{mitra2026behe}. It is a useful resource and it makes the same point this
thesis makes, which is that spontaneous code-mixed speech is missing from the
existing corpora, but it is not spontaneous speech itself. The Indic work is real
speech but targets the native script \cite{chadha2022indic}. The community model
\texttt{whisper-small-benglish} is the closest in spirit, since its card names
lecture notes among its intended uses, but it is a model card rather than a study.
It is trained on about 1.6 hours and evaluated on a random ten per cent of the same
chunks, so the same speakers and very likely the same recordings appear on both
sides of the split, and no example output is given, so the orthography it produces
cannot be confirmed. A public repository by Asrarul applies LoRA to
Whisper-large-v3 for Bengali-English meeting speech and is the closest match in
method, but it reports no results.

The honest conclusion is therefore narrower than ``the first Banglish speech
recogniser''. Bengali-English code-switched speech recognition exists. What does
not exist, as far as could be found, is a system that transcribes spontaneous
Bengali-English classroom speech into romanized Banglish, evaluated on whole
lectures held out from training, on hand-checked references, with the whiteboard
read alongside it. Every ``first'' sentence in this thesis is written in that
form.

\subsection{How code-switched speech recognition is measured}

The evaluation problem is a recognised research topic in its own right, and it is
directly relevant because two separate results in this thesis were affected by it.

Hamed et al. benchmarked evaluation metrics for code-switching ASR against human
judgements and found that transliteration followed by text normalisation gives the
strongest agreement with human acceptance \cite{hamed2022benchmarking}. Emond et
al. proposed transliteration-based word error rate for the same reason, mapping
mixed-script output into a single script before scoring
\cite{emond2018transliteration}. PolyWER goes further and accepts several valid
forms of a reference, including transliterations and translations, arguing that
plain word error rate is simply too strict for speech that can be correctly written
in more than one way \cite{kadaoui2024polywer}.

The common thread is that a measure built around one script or one vocabulary will
misjudge a system that legitimately produces another. This thesis reaches the same
conclusion from a different direction. Its inherited term-based measure counts a
fixed list of English words, so a model that transcribes the mixed speech
faithfully emits fewer of them than a model that translates, and scores worse for
being right. The effect is quantified in Chapter 5 and is one of the findings
offered as a contribution.

These systems solve a closely related problem and solve it well. Two differences
matter for this thesis. First, their output is a binarised summary image or an
index of handwriting regions, which is the right target when the goal is search
and navigation, whereas the target here is a photograph-like board that a student
can look at inside a note, with the original strokes and colours intact. Second,
none of them attempts to read the content into text with a model that understands
what it is reading, because that capability did not exist when most of them were
published.

The board method used here is a tiled mosaic over erase-separated eras, which is
engineering built from known parts rather than a new algorithm, and Chapter 4
describes it as such. Its one property worth defending is that nothing is
generated. Every pixel of an output board is unmodified camera output taken from
some real frame, so there is no possibility of the system inventing writing.
Generative inpainting was rejected for exactly this reason, since a plausible
invented character on a board is worse than a visible gap.

\subsection{Reading text in images with vision-language models}

The step this thesis adds on top of board reconstruction is a vision-language
model that reads the board. Modern open models are strong at text in images and are
routinely benchmarked on document understanding tasks such as DocVQA, OCRBench and
TextVQA \cite{bai2025qwen25vl}. Handwriting is harder than print, and recent
benchmarks target it directly, which is a reminder that a high score on printed
documents does not transfer automatically to a whiteboard.

Because this is a young area, results are strongly affected by the prompt, as the
prompt-sensitivity study cited earlier shows \cite{ismithdeen2025promptception}.
This thesis reports a large instance of that effect and treats it as a finding
rather than as a tuning detail.

\subsection{Automatic lecture notes and multimodal summarisation}

Turning a recorded lecture into notes is an old goal that has been given new life
by large models. M3AV is a recent academic lecture dataset of nearly 367 hours
across five sources with human annotation of slide text and spoken words, built to
support contextual speech recognition, slide generation and script generation
\cite{chen2024m3av}. NoteIt converts instructional videos into interactable notes
by extracting hierarchical structure and multimodal key information, and evaluates
the result both on technical metrics and with a user study of 36 participants
\cite{zhao2025noteit}.

Two gaps stand out when this work is put beside the classroom this thesis targets.
The first is the visual source. Existing systems assume slides, and slides are text
that can be read reliably with optical character recognition or taken directly
from the file. Our lecturers write on a whiteboard and are standing in front of
what they write. The second is language. These datasets and systems are built for
English or Chinese, and for speech that stays in one language.

The user study in NoteIt also points at something this thesis has not done. Whether
notes are good is a question about readers, and it is answered by asking readers.
Chapter 6 lists the reader study as the main piece of future work, and Chapter 5 is
careful never to claim readability from a metric that cannot see it.

\section{Summary of Key Findings}

The review supports four conclusions.

\textbf{The speech problem is real and is not solved by anything available.}
Bengali speech recognition is mature but writes the wrong script for this purpose.
Code-switched recognition for Bengali and English exists, but the published
systems either translate into English, are trained on synthetic speech, or target
the native script. The one community model that matches the intent most closely is
undocumented and is evaluated on a random split of its own clips rather than on
unseen recordings.

\textbf{The board problem is well studied, and this thesis should not claim
otherwise.} Extracting handwritten content from fixed-camera lecture video has a
fifteen-year literature with strong results on public datasets. What is new here is
not the extraction but what happens next, which is reading the reconstructed board
with a vision-language model and carrying numbered regions of it into a generated
note.

\textbf{Measurement is a genuine open problem in this area, not a detail.} Several
independent groups have found that standard word error rate misjudges
code-switched output, and have proposed transliteration-based or multi-reference
alternatives. The same class of problem appears twice in this thesis, once in the
inherited term measure and once in the note-quality measure, and reporting it
honestly is part of the contribution.

\textbf{Lecture note generation with large models is active but assumes a
different classroom.} Existing systems expect slides and a single language.
Nothing found combines code-mixed speech, a handwritten whiteboard and generated
notes in two languages.
