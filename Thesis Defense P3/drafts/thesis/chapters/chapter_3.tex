Seven non-functional requirements constrain how those functions are delivered. The system
runs on one consumer graphics card, with speech training fitting in 12 GB using gradient
checkpointing and the vision-language work in 32 GB, and uses no cluster and no paid
cloud service. It is reproducible: every reported number has a command that regenerates
it, and splits, seeds and chosen settings are written to disk beside the model. It is
restartable, because each stage writes its output to a file and is skipped when that file
exists. Nothing in the board image is fabricated, since every pixel comes from a real
frame and generative inpainting is not used. Claims in the note are traceable, because
quotations are checked against the transcript, board content is supplied as text and non
lecture material is labelled. The system runs offline once the model weights are
downloaded, so no lecture audio, video or text is sent to an external service. Finally,
recordings and transcripts are held in a private repository and the public output of the
project contains no raw recording.

\subsection{Data requirements}

Three kinds of data were needed, and the cost of producing them shaped the schedule.

Forty-four classroom recordings were collected from three lecturers, 8.33 hours in total,
each a fixed camera view of a lecturer at a whiteboard with no slides. The subjects are
introductory programming, digital logic, databases and computer networking.

Twenty-eight of these lectures, covering 5.15 hours of speech in 609 timed segments and
about 39,100 words, were transcribed by hand. Every transcript follows the written
standard of Appendix A and is validated by a checking script. The remaining sixteen
recordings have no transcript and support the vision side and the demonstration only.

To measure whether board content reaches the notes, the contents of 45 boards were
written out by hand as lists of items, 436 items in total, each labelled as a number,
name, code fragment, term or phrase. These records are used only for scoring and are
never given to a model. A separate human check covered a further 98 boards and recorded
whether anything written was missing from the reconstruction.

\subsection{Hardware, software and constraints}

All speech work ran on a desktop with an RTX 3060 of 12 GB, covering data preparation,
hyperparameter tuning, LoRA training, evaluation, board reconstruction and the person
segmentation network. All Qwen work ran on a second desktop with an RTX 5090 of 32 GB,
covering board reading, box naming and note generation for every lecture. The first
version of the pipeline, whose results are reported separately where they are still used,
ran on a third machine with an RTX 3090 of 24 GB.

The software is Python 3.11 with PyTorch 2.5.1 for CUDA 12.1 on the first desktop and
Python 3.12 with PyTorch 2.11 for CUDA 12.8 on the second, which the newer card requires.
Both run Transformers 5.12.1 with PEFT for the LoRA adapters, and FFmpeg extracts audio
and frames. The models are whisper-large-v3-turbo for speech, Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct and
Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct for board reading, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct for note writing, a
pretrained DeepLabV3 with a ResNet-101 backbone for person segmentation
\cite{chen2017deeplabv3}, and a WavLM speaker embedding model used only to confirm which
lecturer appears in which recording. About 31 GB of model weights and 75 GB of video have
to be stored.

Four constraints were fixed. The corpus is what three lecturers agreed to record and what
the team could transcribe by hand in the time available, which bounds how strong any
speech result can be. Training the 809 million parameter speech model on a 12 GB card is
possible only with gradient checkpointing, and the largest vision-language models were
out of reach, since Qwen2.5-VL-32B is 68 GB of weights and the 72B version 147 GB. The
project runs to a fixed submission date, so a fourteen hour run can be started once
rather than five times. The fourth constraint was self imposed and removed the most work:
no number enters this report unless a command in the project regenerates it. An earlier
version of the project carried figures whose numbers had been typed rather than computed,
and those were removed rather than defended.

\section{Societal Impact}

The immediate beneficiaries are students in classrooms where teaching is code-mixed. A
student who missed a class, who is slower at English, or who wants to revise currently has
a video and nothing else, and the system turns that video into a document containing the
board. Because the note exists in Banglish as well as English, a student who thinks in the
mixed language is not forced to read a translation of their own lecture. Lecturers also
gain a record of every board they wrote, which nothing else in a whiteboard class
preserves and which costs them no extra work.

Language technology serves widely spoken standard languages well and mixed speech badly,
so a student whose classroom language is Bengali-English code-mixed is poorly served by
both English tools and Bengali tools. Treating that mixture as a first class target, with
its own transcription standard and its own written form, is a step against that imbalance,
and the same argument applies to the choice of the Roman alphabet. A text transcript and a
text version of the board also make a lecture reachable by a screen reader and searchable
by keyword, which a video is not, although the system was not designed as an assistive
technology and has not been tested with users who have a disability.

Three risks deserve naming. Generated notes are not guaranteed to be correct, and Chapter
5 gives a real example in which the model wrote that the two universal gates are XOR gates
when the lecturer and the board both said NAND and NOR. A student who trusts such a note
will learn the wrong thing, so any deployment must present the notes as machine generated
study aids to be checked against the lecture, and the system keeps the board image beside
the text so that a reader can check. A lecture recording also captures a person at work
and may capture student voices; this project used recordings made with the lecturers'
permission and kept them private, and any wider use needs consent from the people
recorded. Finally, good automatic notes may reduce attendance, and taking notes by hand
has a learning value of its own. That trade-off is not resolved here, and the system is
aimed at revision and at students who could not attend rather than at replacing
attendance.

There is no physical safety dimension to the work. Lecture recordings and whiteboard
content belong to the lecturer and the university, so recordings are not redistributed and
released model weights are used under their own licences. Culturally, the system preserves
the lecturer's own mixed speech rather than converting it into standard English, which
respects how the class is taught.

\section{Environmental Impact}

Training machine learning models consumes electricity, and the field has been criticised
for not reporting it \cite{strubell2019energy}. The headline figures in that literature
come from very large models trained on clusters. The work in this thesis sits at the
other end of the scale, and this section gives the actual figure rather than claiming the
question does not apply.

fine-tuned end to end. Table \ref{tab:compute} lists the machine time taken by the jobs

The energy figure below is an estimate from the measured hours and stated assumptions
rather than a meter reading. Assuming a whole system draw of about 250 W for the first
desktop while training and about 600 W for the second, the 18 hours on the first machine
and 6 hours on the second come to roughly 4.5 and 3.6 kilowatt hours, so about 8 kWh in
total. At a grid emission factor of about 0.65 kilograms of carbon dioxide equivalent per
kilowatt hour, a reasonable figure for the Bangladesh grid, that is roughly 5 kilograms
of carbon dioxide equivalent for the entire experimental programme. The widely cited
estimate for training a single large language model with neural architecture search is
over 280,000 kilograms \cite{strubell2019energy}.

Three decisions kept the figure small, and each was taken for other reasons as well.
Using LoRA rather than full fine-tuning means a training run updates a few million
parameters instead of 809 million, so a run finishes in about an hour on a mid-range
card. Choosing hyperparameters by a plan with a fixed number of stages meant seven
training runs rather than an open ended search. Not running the 32 billion and 72 billion
parameter vision-language models avoided both a 68 GB download and the compute to use
them, and the comparison in Section 5.2.3 shows the larger model was not where the
important difference lay.

In use the system runs on one desktop, and processing a lecture end to end takes a few
minutes of graphics card time, so a department could run it on hardware it already owns.
There is no requirement for a data centre and no per-lecture payment to an external
service, which also means no lecture content leaves the institution.

\section{Ethical Issues}

Every recording used here was made with the knowledge and permission of the lecturer in
it. Three lecturers took part. The recordings are not published and are not shared
outside the team. Where a board image appears in this report, the lecturer has been
removed by the reconstruction itself, so the figures show writing rather than people.
Videos, audio, transcripts and board images are kept in a private repository, the
transcripts contain classroom speech only, and no student personal data was collected. No
lecture content is sent to an external service at any point, because every model runs
locally, which was one reason for preferring open model weights over a commercial
interface.

The most serious professional risk in a project of this kind is reporting a number that
was never computed, and this project had that problem in its earlier phase. A
significance value, an effect size, an ablation table and a set of per-video statistics
had been written into figures and text without any code behind them. They were found
during a review, they are listed in the project record as retired claims, and none of
them appears in this report. Two rules were adopted afterwards and kept: a number may be
written only if a command in the project regenerates it, and results that go against the
project's own hypothesis are reported in the same detail as results that support it.
Chapter 5 contains several of the second kind.

A note generator can also state things the lecturer never said. The design contains three
controls for this, described in Section 4.4.4, and Section 5.3 reports how well each one
held, including the counted number of quotations that had to be deleted and the one box
reference that survived the checker. The controls reduce the risk without removing it.
There is an academic integrity dimension as well: notes produced by a machine from a
lecture are a study aid, and if a student submits them as their own writing that is
plagiarism in the ordinary sense, which is one reason generated material inside the notes
is labelled.

\section{Project Management Plan}

The work ran in five phases. The first built and measured the earlier version of the
pipeline, which established that the speech side was the binding problem and that note
quality had never been evaluated. The second checked every claim of that phase against
code, retired the claims with no computation behind them and introduced the rule that a
number needs a command. The third produced the data: the transcription standard, the
recordings and the hand-checked transcripts. This phase took the most human hours and ran
in parallel with the others. The fourth ran the experiments, with speech work on one
machine and vision-language work on the other so that neither waited for the other. The
fifth produced the figures, tables and this report.

\figplaceholder{fig-3-1-gantt}{Project schedule.}{A Gantt chart with one row per phase,

Three students worked on the project under a supervisor and a co-supervisor. The
resources were the two desktops described in Section 3.1.3, both already owned, the open
model weights and the team's own time; no budget was spent on cloud computing or paid
annotation. Two practices mattered more than any tool. Long jobs were written as scripts
that run unattended, commit their own results and push them, so a fourteen hour training
run cost no attention. All planning lived in two files in the repository, one holding the
ordered task list and one holding every result with the command that produces it, so work
could move between machines without anything being carried in a person's memory.

The fixed points were the draft submission and the slide submission, and work was
scheduled backwards from them. When the corpus was closed at 5.15 hours the planned six
hour and ten hour training runs were cancelled rather than delayed, because a result that
arrives after the deadline is worth nothing.

\section{Economic Analysis}

The project spent no money on computing. Both desktops were already available and every
model used is published under an open licence, so the real cost is human time, and most
of it is transcription. Transcribing 5.15 hours of lecture speech by hand took about 31
person-hours at roughly one hour of work per ten minutes of video. Writing and verifying
the 45 board records, 436 items, took about one working day, and checking 98 rebuilt
boards for missing writing took 30 to 40 minutes. The graphics card time for every
reported experiment was about 24 hours, which is about 8 kWh and a negligible cash
amount, and storage came to about 106 GB on existing disks.

Two alternatives are worth pricing. Renting the same compute is cheap: 24 hours on a
rented mid-range graphics card at typical rates of one to two United States dollars an
hour is about thirty to fifty dollars, so compute is not what makes a project of this
kind expensive. Commercial transcription is the opposite. Bengali speech-to-text services
exist, but they output the Bengali script, so for this purpose they would not remove any
of the human work and would add a fee on top.

The benefit to be weighed is the reduction in human effort to produce a usable
transcript. Producing one by hand from nothing took about six hours of work per hour of
video, because off-the-shelf output was useless as a starting point. The fine-tuned model
reaches a median character error rate of 15.8 per cent, which is a draft a person can
correct rather than retype. If correcting such a draft takes a third of the time, the
cost falls from about six hours per hour of video to about two, which over the 8.33 hours
recorded here is the difference between about fifty hours of work and about seventeen.
This projection has not been tested and is not claimed as a result; measuring it properly
means timing people as they correct real drafts, which is listed with the reader study in
Chapter 6. What can be claimed is the input to the calculation, which is the measured
error rate and the measured cost of the manual route.

A department wanting to run this on its own lectures would need one desktop with a
graphics card of 12 GB or more, which is ordinary teaching laboratory hardware, and no
recurring payment. Processing one lecture takes a few minutes of card time. The recurring
cost is storage for the recordings and somebody to check the notes before they reach
students, and that check is the part the system does not remove.
