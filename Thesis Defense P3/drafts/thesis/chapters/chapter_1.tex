\section{Background}

Teaching in Bangladeshi universities is rarely monolingual. In a typical computer science
class the lecturer explains an idea in Bangla, names it in English, writes the English
term on the whiteboard and returns to Bangla for the worked example. A single sentence
carries both languages, as in \textit{erpor e ami ki korbo? not korbo}, where the grammar
is Bengali and the technical word is English. Students almost never write this mixture in
the Bengali script. They type it in Roman letters, and the result is commonly called
Banglish. Because students take notes, message each other and search in Banglish, it is
the written form that matches how the class actually sounds.

Automatic speech recognition performs well on the languages it was built for. Whisper is
trained on roughly 680,000 hours of multilingual audio and reaches usable accuracy on
many languages, including Bengali \cite{radford2023whisper}. Such models assume, however,
that an utterance belongs to one language. Whisper receives a language token and a task
token, and on Bengali audio containing English words it usually produces a fluent English
translation instead of a transcript. A Bengali model trained on clean Bengali writes
everything in Bengali script and spells the English technical terms phonetically. Neither
output preserves the lecturer's words, and neither can be searched by a student who
writes in Banglish.

Speech is also only half of a whiteboard lecture. The classrooms recorded for this work
use no slides. Definitions, truth tables, SQL queries, subnetting calculations and
diagrams appear on the board and are then discussed, so a note taken from the audio alone
loses them. Recovering the board from the video is not a screenshot problem either. The
lecturer stands in front of what he has just written, the ceiling lights leave glare on
the surface, and the board is wiped and reused several times in one class.

Three developments make a system that handles both sides practical on ordinary hardware.
Parameter efficient fine-tuning, in particular Low-Rank Adaptation \cite{hu2022lora},
adapts a large speech model on a few hours of new speech by training a small number of
additional weights, which fits in the memory of one desktop graphics card. Open
vision-language models such as Qwen2.5-VL read text inside an image without a dedicated
optical character recognition stage \cite{bai2025qwen25vl}. Instruction tuned language
models such as Qwen2.5-7B can take a transcript and board content together and write a
structured document from them \cite{qwen2025qwen25}.

This thesis combines the three into a system called InsightLens. A lecture video is the
input. The output is a lecture note containing the board redrawn without the lecturer in
front of it, numbered coloured boxes around each part of the board, an explanation
written from the lecturer's own words, and quotations checked against the transcript. The
note is produced in Banglish and in English so that the student can choose.

\section{Rational of the Study or Motivation}

A student who misses a class, or who cannot follow the English terms at the speed they
are spoken, currently has only the recording. Three hours of video is not a study aid.
Passing that video through an available transcription service returns either English
prose that was never spoken or Bengali script that nobody in the class writes, and
neither contains the whiteboard.

The second motivation is cost. Producing a correct Banglish transcript by hand took about
one hour of work for every ten minutes of video in this project, because existing tools
give a transcriber nothing usable to start from. At that rate one course for one semester
is several weeks of work. Bringing a model close enough to the lecturer's words that a
person only corrects small errors would reduce that cost substantially, which is why the
error rate of the speech model is treated here as a result in its own right rather than
as an internal detail.

The third motivation is that the gap is specific and checkable. Code switched speech
recognition has been studied for Hindi-English and several other pairs, and Bengali
speech recognition has its own literature. What is missing is the combination a
Bangladeshi classroom needs, which is recognition of Bengali-English code-mixed speech
written back in the Roman alphabet and evaluated on real lectures and not on read or
synthetic speech. Chapter 2 reviews the systems that come closest and states which part
of this work is new.

The whiteboard side has value beyond note taking. A clean picture of every board a
lecturer wrote, with the person removed and the content available as text, can be
searched and reused, and it is a record of a class that was otherwise never written down.

\section{Problem Statement}

Given an ordinary recording of a university lecture delivered in Bengali-English
code-mixed speech, in a room with a whiteboard and no slides, produce a lecture note that
is faithful to what the lecturer said and to what the lecturer wrote.

Four smaller problems make this difficult, and each was confirmed by measurement on our
own recordings before any solution was attempted. The first is that existing systems do
not transcribe the speech. On the six lectures held out for testing,
off-the-shelf Whisper large-v3-turbo produces a median character error rate of 67.7 per
cent and a median word error rate of 93.9 per cent against human references. The failure
is systematic, not random. The model translates into English and, on thirteen of
the 177 test clips, repeats a phrase until it reaches the token limit. Table
\ref{tab:motivating_example} shows one segment of a digital logic class in three
versions.

The second is that the person who wrote on the board is standing in front of it. Measured over 79 frames of
one lecture, the lecturer covers 23.5 per cent of the writing surface in the median frame
and 7.0 per cent in the clearest one, and no frame is below 5 per cent. There is no
moment at which a clean photograph of the board can simply be taken.

The third is that a summarising model writes from memory instead of from the
lecture. The first version of
this pipeline asked a language model for clear notes on the lecture, and the model
produced textbook material, including a definition of a NAND gate that the lecturer never
gave and that was wrong. Scored against hand-checked records of what was written on the
board, those notes contained 37.2 per cent of the board items across 35 boards, and only
six of the 67 numbers.

The last is that the obvious ways of scoring the system are misleading. A term based F1 over a fixed list
of 82 English words, inherited from the earlier phase of this project, moves by 8.1
percentage points between two training runs on identical data, and it moves in the wrong
direction. A model that transcribes the mixed speech correctly emits fewer English words
than a model that translates it, so the better transcript scores worse. Any measure built
from English strings behaves this way, and part of the contribution of this thesis is to
document it.

\section{Objective}

The aim is to build and evaluate a system that converts a recorded Banglish whiteboard
lecture into a usable lecture note, and to report how well each part of it works. The
specific objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item to build a hand-checked corpus of Bengali-English code-mixed classroom speech,
        transcribed in the Roman alphabet against a written standard, large enough to
        fine-tune and test a speech model;
  \item to adapt a pretrained speech recognition model to this speech with Low-Rank
        Adaptation and measure the change in error rate on lectures never used for
        training;
  \item to choose the training settings by a procedure written down before any result is
        seen and scored on a separate validation set, so that the reported test result is
        not the product of repeated tuning on the test data;
  \item to rebuild each whiteboard from the video without the lecturer in front of it and
        without synthesising any pixel, and to measure how much of the writing survives;
  \item to have a vision-language model read each rebuilt board and measure how much of
        its content is recovered against records checked by hand;
  \item to generate a lecture note in Banglish and English that is grounded in the board
        and the transcript, points at numbered regions of the board image, and quotes the
        lecturer only in words that appear in the transcript;
  \item to evaluate each stage with paired statistical tests and ablations, and to report
        the approaches that did not work as fully as the ones that did.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology in Brief}

The system runs as four stages. Each stage writes a file that the next stage reads, so
any stage can be re-run or replaced on its own.

The first stage handles the speech. Audio is extracted from the video and cut into
segments at the boundaries given by the transcription standard. Whisper large-v3-turbo is
adapted with LoRA adapters on the training lectures, and the adapted model writes a
timestamped Banglish transcript for a lecture it has not seen.

The second stage handles the board. Frames are taken at a fixed interval and the points
at which the board is wiped are detected, which divides the lecture into eras with one
board per era. Within an era the frame is cut into overlapping tiles and each tile is
taken from a moment when the lecturer was not standing in front of that tile. The
lecturer is located by a pretrained person segmentation network together with a shadow
mask, so every pixel of the result is unmodified camera output.

The third stage reads the board. Our own code finds the blocks of writing, draws a
numbered coloured box around each, and a vision-language model names and transcribes each
box. This follows the Set-of-Mark style of prompting \cite{yang2023som}, in which the
marks are drawn on the image and the model answers per mark.

The fourth stage writes the note. For each board a language model receives that board's
boxes, the board text and the part of the transcript belonging to that board, and returns
one section of notes that refers to the boxes by number and colour. A checker then
deletes any quotation absent from the transcript and any reference to a box that does not
exist, and the sections are assembled into a page in Markdown and HTML.

Evaluation is done per stage. Speech is scored by per-clip median word and character
error rate against human transcripts with paired Wilcoxon signed-rank and sign tests. The
board reconstruction is scored by the fraction of tiles that found a clear view and by a
human check of whether any writing was lost. Board reading and note quality are scored by
recall of items from hand-verified records of each board's content.

\section{Scopes and Challenges}

The dataset is 44 lecture recordings from three lecturers at one university, 8.33 hours
of video, of which 28 lectures covering 5.15 hours carry hand-checked transcripts. The
subjects are introductory programming, digital logic, databases and computer networking.
Every recording is a whiteboard class with no slides, taken with a fixed camera. Notes
are produced in Banglish and English. Notes in the Bengali script were considered and
dropped, since they would be produced by translation alone and would add nothing the
system itself contributes. The system is built for recorded lectures, not live captioning.

Two limits are stated at the outset. The speech model is not accurate enough to be used
without a human check, and Chapter 5 gives the remaining error rate. The generated notes
have not been tested with readers, so nothing here shows that they are good to read. The
measure used for the notes counts whether board content reached the page, which is a
checkable property, but it says nothing about whether the surrounding explanation is well
written or correct.

Five difficulties shaped the work. Banglish has no agreed orthography, so a written
transcription guide was produced and enforced by a validator, and a spelling tolerant
version of the error rate was computed to separate spelling disagreement from genuine
error. No public corpus of Bengali-English code-mixed classroom speech in Roman script
exists, so the corpus had to be recorded and transcribed by hand, which was the largest
cost in the project. All training ran on one desktop with a 12 GB graphics card and all
vision-language work on a second with a 32 GB card, which put the largest
vision-language models out of reach. The configuration chosen by the tuning procedure
diverged on one of its two random seeds and produced a model worse than doing nothing.
Finally, measures built from English strings reward a model that translates, which
affects two separate results reported in Chapter 5.
